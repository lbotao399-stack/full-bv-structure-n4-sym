1   Introduction and Summary

The tractability of Supersymmetric Quantum Field Theories (SQFTs) comes from the ex-
istence of Q-closed local operators, i.e. those annihilated by the action of some (set of)
supercharge(s)
                                          QO = 0 .                                   (1.1)
Such operators are useful for understanding SUSY vacua [1, 2], deformations and non-
renormalization theorems [3–6], and for matching across supersymmetric and holographic
dualities [7–13]. Thus, to understand the space of Q-closed operators is to understand the
“superdata” of an SQFT.
    In this paper, we consider the most general set of such operators in (3+1)d SQFTs: the
semi-chiral operators. By definition, semi-chiral operators are annihilated by one (minimal




                                           –1–
rank) nilpotent supercharge, say1
                                                 Q := Q1− .                                              (1.2)
Correlation functions of semi-chiral operators are regular and insensitive to changes by Q-
exact quantities in SUSY vacua. Consequently, it is useful to pass to Q-cohomology, turning
our attention to the “semi-chiral ring” of the SQFT – a holomorphic generalization of the
chiral ring.
    In this note, we describe a systematic procedure for computing the quantum corrected
semi-chiral ring of interacting SQFTs using a perturbative expansion:

                           Q = Qfree + Qtree + ℏQ1-loop + ℏ2 Q2-loop + . . . ,                           (1.3)

based on the holomorphic twist formalism of [16–19].
     Before discussing SQFTs, it is instructive to consider an analog of these ideas in Confor-
mal Field Theory (CFT). In CFTs, the scaling dimensions ∆i and three-point functions Cijk
of primary local operators make up the entirety of the conformal data. By the state-operator
correspondence, understanding the space of local operators also tells us about all states in the
(vacuum sector of the) QFT, so enumerating the space of local operators is as complicated
as computing the partition function of the CFT on S 1 × S d .
     Quantum corrections to the action of the dilatation operator D on local operators are
described by anomalies/anomalous dimensions. In perturbation theory, we construct com-
posite local operators from normal ordered product of free fields, i.e. given fields ϕ1 and ϕ2 ,
we can construct the normal-ordered primary :ϕ1 ϕ2 :, and so on with derivatives. The free
dilatation operator Dfree acts on normal-ordered products by the Leibniz rule, so :ϕ1 ϕ2: has
classical free scaling dimension ∆1 + ∆2 . However, in the interacting quantum theory we can
have multiple Wick contractions, leading to loop diagrams and thus anomalous dimensions

                                 D :ϕ1 ϕ2: = (∆1 + ∆2 + γ12 (ℏ)) :ϕ1 ϕ2: ,                               (1.4)

i.e., operator renormalization generically prevents D from acting by the Leibniz rule.
      The connection between modified actions of generators, anomalies, and quantum cor-
rected equations of motion is neither exotic nor limited to D. Even the action of translations
on operators can receive quantum corrections. For example, any symmetry current J µ is clas-
sically conserved Pµ J µ = 0 using the equations of motion, but can be non-conserved quantum
mechanically. For chiral fermions in a representation R, the action of Pµ on the current J5µ
famously receives corrections at one-loop

2        The Holomorphic Twist and Corrections to Q from Anomalies

In this section we define the basic objects that we will use in the next section of the paper. In
Section 2.1, we introduce semi-chiral superfields and the holomorphic twist used for computa-
tions in the rest of the paper. In Section 2.2, we explain how corrections to the action of Q on
local operators should be understood as a “twice generalized Konishi anomaly,” by reviewing
the famous Konishi anomaly corrections to the chiral ring [2] and then the extension to the
semi-chiral ring via the holomorphic twist developed in [16–18]. After reviewing this back-
ground and justifying the relationship to anomalies, we explain the extremely constrained
form of loop corrections in cohomological holomorphic(-topological) QFTs, and thus to the
action of Q on operators, in Section 2.3.

2.2     Corrections to Q from Anomalies
In the previous section, we explained how semi-chiral operators and their descendants are
described by a cohomological holomorphic QFT, obtained as the Q-cohomology of the original
theory. Here, we will explain how corrections to Q can be understood as anomalies in SQFTs,
and how these anomalies are neatly captured (and very rigidly constrained) by the “higher
operations” of the holomorphic twist, see also [16, 17, 19, 22, 56].

2.2.1    Warmup: The Generalized Konishi Anomaly and the Chiral Ring
One of the most well-known examples of quantum corrections to Q comes from considering
the chiral ring in supersymmetric gauge theories, and the modification of equivalence relations
by the Konishi anomaly [2, 20, 21].

SYM is classically scale invariant and has a U (1)R symmetry, but both of these are broken
by one-loop corrections. Consequently, Sα̇ is in the classical Q0 -cohomology, but only ∂ α̇ Sα̇
is expected in the quantum cohomology. As it turns out, ∂ α̇ Sα̇ is not just Q1 -closed, but
Q1 -exact, leaving the holomorphic twist of pure SYM completely topological.

3.4.1      Loop Corrections in the Planar Limit
Here, we discuss the one-loop corrections presented in the previous section in the planar limit
of N = 4 SYM with gauge group SU (N ).25
    Due to the Lie algebra factors fACD fBCE , the one-loop supercharge Q1 can join and split
traces. To illustrate this, write one term in the Q1 action schematically as

                                    Q1 (X A Y B ) ∼ fACD fBCE U D V E .                               (3.71)

For simplicity, X, Y, U are bosonic and V is fermionic.
     Now we consider the action of Q1 on neighbouring and non-neighbouring letters X, Y
inside an example single-trace:

                    Q1 Tr(XY AB) ∼ −N Tr(U V AB) − Tr(U V ) Tr(AB)                                    (3.72)
                                        + Tr(U ) Tr(V AB) + Tr(V ) Tr(U AB) + . . .                   (3.73)
                    Q1 Tr(XAY B) ∼ Tr(U A) Tr(V B) + Tr(U B) Tr(V A)                                  (3.74)
                                        − Tr(A) Tr(U V B) − Tr(B) Tr(U AV ) + . . . ,                 (3.75)

where the ellipses denote terms where Q1 acts on other pairs of letters. We see that Q1 acting
on neighboring letters in a single-trace can produce a single-trace output and a multi-trace
output. Similarly, Q1 can join traces. Acting on X in the first trace and Y in the second
trace gives

                      Q1 (Tr(XA) Tr(Y B)) ∼ Tr(U AV B) + Tr(AU BV )
                                                − Tr(U ABV ) − Tr(AU V B) + . . . .                   (3.76)

    We would like to study the action of Q1 on the infinite-N single-trace tree-level cohomol-
ogy, which is in one-to-one correspondence with the 1/16-BPS spectrum of N = 4 SYM. The
